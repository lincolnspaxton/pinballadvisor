Buying guide

Before you buy a pinball machine

Pro, Premium, LE: what you actually get

Most new games ship in three trims. The rules and theme stay the same; the toys and finish change. For a home game that guests will play, a Pro is usually enough. If you'll be the main player for years, the extra mechs on a Premium are where the price jump tends to pay off.

ProBase toys, standard cabinet art, often a simpler playfield.Lowest price. Easiest to resell.
PremiumExtra mechs and ramps, upgraded lighting, sometimes extra modes.The usual sweet spot for owners.
LEPremium playfield plus numbered run, special art and trim.Collector pricing. Pay for rarity, not gameplay.

New vs. used

New games come with a warranty and a distributor to call. Used games cost less up front but need a careful look. A clean, shopped classic from the 1990s can hold its value better than a new game that loses a chunk the day it's delivered.

What to check on a used game

  • Play several full games. Every flipper, pop bumper and slingshot should fire strong on both sides.
  • Run the switch and coil tests from the service menu and note anything that fails.
  • Look under the playfield for burnt boards, melted connectors, or hacked wiring.
  • Check playfield wear in front of the flippers and around scoops. Clear-coated is good; bare wood is a cost.
  • Ask for the serial number and whether manuals, keys and the original backglass come with it.

Getting it home

With the legs off and the head folded down, most modern games fit through a standard 30-inch doorway on their side using a pinball dolly. Never carry it by the backbox. Stairs are a two-to-three-person job; many sellers and techs offer delivery for a fee and it's usually worth it.

Setting it up

Use a level app on the lockdown bar: side to side should read level, front to back around 6.5 degrees of pitch. Put it on its own outlet if you can, and keep it out of direct sunlight, which fades art fast.

Service & repair

Keep it flipping

Most home games need a little attention every few hundred plays. Here's the routine, the usual problems, and a way to book help.

Routine maintenance

Common problems

A flipper feels weak

Check the end-of-stroke switch or flipper board, then look at the coil sleeve and plunger for wear. A worn sleeve is a cheap part and the most common cause.

The ball gets stuck

Usually a ramp that has shifted, a sticky scoop, or a lane guide that came loose. Note exactly where it stops; that location is the fix.

A switch doesn't register

Run switch test from the service menu. If the switch never shows, check for a broken wire or diode. If it shows intermittently, the rollover or opto may need adjusting.

Lights or display are out

Check fuses first, then connectors on the light board. On older games, burnt header pins on power connectors are a classic failure.

The game reboots during play

Often low voltage from a tired power supply or a bad ground. Stop playing and have it checked; repeated resets can damage boards.
